## Rules

### Every control comes in three tiers, and the field frame belongs to the middle one

A control family is named after its field tier: `TextInput` → `TextField` → `TSFTextField`. Pick the lowest one that does the job.

1. **The raw compound**, such as `TextInput` or `Select`, owns the control and nothing else. It has no label, description or error, and it does not lay itself out. Take it whenever the anatomy differs from an ordinary field: a control in a toolbar, two controls sharing one group, a label beside rather than above. Its `Label` part still names the control.
2. **The shorthand**, such as `TextField`, assembles the raw parts with `Field` into the ordinary case: a label above a full-width control, then the description, then the error. It is built from the parts, never a second implementation of them, and it takes no prop that switches it to another anatomy. A control that should not be a stacked field drops to the raw tier.
3. **The bridge**, such as `TSFTextField`, wires the shorthand to TanStack Form. It is registered in the form hook an app builds with `createAppForm` and reached as `field.TextField` inside `form.AppField`, so a call site never types its prefix.

### Every field takes the same props on every tier

`label`, `description`, `errors`, `requiredness`, `optionalLabel`, `dirty`, `touched` and `pending`, with the same meaning on the shorthand and the bridge. A bridge passes them at the top level. The raw root takes the states among them, `requiredness` included; the label, the description and the requiredness suffix are parts placed inside it.

```tsx
<form.AppField name="email">
	{field => (
		<field.EmailField
			label="Email"
			description="We send the receipt here."
			requiredness="required"
			optionalLabel="optional"
		/>
	)}
</form.AppField>
```

### Requiredness is stated, never inferred

`requiredness` is `"required"`, `"optional"`, or left out, and left out shows neither mark. Nothing reads it from a schema and there is no default. A required label carries the mark, which is decoration: the control's own `required` tells assistive tech. An optional label trails `optionalLabel`, which is part of the control's name.

### A label renders its children, and the suffix is a part

The shorthand adds the suffix for you. On the raw tier, a label renders only its children, so the suffix is `Field.Requiredness`, placed inside the label after its text. It reads `requiredness` from the root it sits in, or from its own prop where no root carries it, and takes the `optionalLabel` it shows.

```tsx
<TextInput.Root
	value={value}
	onValueChange={setValue}
	requiredness="optional">
	<TextInput.Label>
		Notes <Field.Requiredness optionalLabel="optional" />
	</TextInput.Label>
	…
</TextInput.Root>
```

A label beside its control, such as a lone checkbox's, keeps the required mark with its text and puts the optional word on a row of its own: `Field.RequiredMark` goes inside `Field.InlineLabelText`, and `Field.InlineOptional` follows it.

```tsx
<Checkbox.Root requiredness="optional">
	<Checkbox.Label>
		<Checkbox.Control>…</Checkbox.Control>
		<Field.InlineLabelText>
			Send me a copy <Field.RequiredMark />
		</Field.InlineLabelText>
		<Field.InlineOptional optionalLabel="optional" />
	</Checkbox.Label>
</Checkbox.Root>
```

### The error is the field's only live region, after the description

`Field.Error` takes errors in any shape `normalizeFieldErrors` accepts: strings, and anything with a string `message`, such as a Standard Schema issue. It renders `ErrorText` as an alert, after `Field.Description`, and both chain into the control's `aria-describedby`. A control never announces the same message a second time, and any line of error makes the field invalid.

### A wait inside a field is a spinner inlay, and the field says it is busy

A field waiting on background work, such as an async validator, mounts `Field.Pending` once the wait is worth showing, and projects `pending` through `fieldStateAttributes`, which sets `aria-busy`. `Field.Pending` is the inlay; the spinner is its child, and is hidden from assistive tech. A raw tier's `Pending` mounts itself once its root's wait is worth showing.

```tsx
<TextInput.Pending>
	<Spinner size={1} />
</TextInput.Pending>
```

### What sits beside a label shares its row

A link or a counter that belongs beside a label, such as "Forgot password?" or `TextInput.Counter`, goes in `Field.LabelRow` with the label, in place of the bare label. The row keeps the label at the start and the rest at the end, and wraps on a narrow field. Every raw tier takes it, and in a field group it takes the label's row. It reads `--field-label-row-gap` and `--field-label-row-align`.

```tsx
<TextInput.Root
	value={password}
	onValueChange={setPassword}>
	<Field.LabelRow>
		<TextInput.Label>Password</TextInput.Label>
		<Link href="/reset">Forgot password?</Link>
	</Field.LabelRow>
	<TextInput.Group>
		<TextInput.Input type="password" />
	</TextInput.Group>
</TextInput.Root>
```

### Every control reads its states through `useFieldState`

`useFieldState` reads each state from its owner: invalid from the errors, disabled from the control's own prop or a busy `Form.Root`, and pending through `useBusyPhase`, so a wait shows only once it has lasted. A control projects what it returns with `fieldStateAttributes` and never assembles the states by hand. Validity is one state, `"valid"` or `"invalid"`, so a field can never claim both.
