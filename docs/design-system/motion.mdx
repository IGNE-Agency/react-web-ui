# Motion

Motion is how a screen shows change. It says where something came from and where it went, that a click was heard, that work is still going on. Done well, nobody notices it. Done badly, it's a wait you sit through, or a flicker that looks like a fault.

<Illustration caption="An ease, drawn as a hill: cut into equal beats of time, it climbs steeply at first and levels out, and the ball comes to rest on the plateau.">
	<MotionDrawing />
</Illustration>

## Time and pace

Every move has two settings: how long it takes, and how it spends that time.

Duration is a cost. Each moment of movement is a moment someone waits, and a task is made of many moves, so the shortest duration that still shows the move is the right one. Small things move quickly and large ones a little slower, because a big surface that flies across the screen feels like a jolt.

Easing is the pace. Nothing in the world starts or stops at full speed: a thing that arrives slows down as it lands, and a thing that leaves gathers speed as it goes. A move at one steady speed looks mechanical. A curve that runs briefly past its end and comes back reads as a small thing popping into place, which is lively once and tiring everywhere.

And some people need less of all of it. Movement across a screen can make people dizzy or sick, so operating systems let them ask for less motion. An interface that ignores the setting puts its animation ahead of their comfort.

## Durations and easings

This product has four durations and five easings, and each has a job. The quickest is for popups, tooltips, hover and press, the next for dialogs, drawers and check marks, and a longer one for large surfaces, like the whole page changing theme. Most moves take the standard curve. A larger move, like a dialog opening, takes the emphasised one. Something arriving slows to a stop, something leaving speeds away, and only a small mark popping in, like a check mark, overshoots.

Every one of them is switched off for someone who has asked for less motion.

## Moving through space

Motion that follows the layout tells people where they are. When tabs sit in a row, the pages behind them form a row too, and moving one tab to the right should feel like moving right. <Pull>A cross-fade, or a slide that always goes the same way, throws away the only spatial claim the tab strip was making.</Pull>

So here, the page one tab to the right arrives from the right and pushes the old one out to the left, and going back reverses both. A page outside the tab strip doesn't slide at all. Someone who asked for less motion, or whose browser can't animate the change, simply gets the new page.

## The art of waiting

People feel time in steps. Under a tenth of a second, a response feels instant. Up to about a second, they notice the delay but keep their train of thought. Past that, they start to wonder whether anything is happening at all.

<Illustration caption="The indicator grows with the wait. An instant wait shows nothing but its result, a short one would only flash the ghost of a spinner, and a long one gets a spinner too big to miss.">
	<WaitingDrawing />
</Illustration>

So an indicator that flashes up for a few frames during a quick refresh helps nobody: it reads as a glitch, not as feedback. But a wait with nothing to show for it is worse, like [a button that only greys out while it works](?path=/docs/components-button--docs#a-button-whose-handler-awaits-carries-a-moving-indicator), or [an empty region that doesn't say whether it's empty or still loading](?path=/docs/design-system-patterns--docs#an-empty-region-says-so-in-its-own-place). <Pull>Every wait owes the person waiting a sign that it's happening.</Pull>

An indicator only indicates while it moves. Movement is the whole signal: a frozen glyph, a bar parked at one width, a placeholder that doesn't breathe, or an animation that has finished all read as an interface that has hung. And its size follows what is waiting: a small one on the line of the control it interrupts, a large one for a whole view.

Here, a busy state waits a moment before it appears, and once it's there it stays for a minimum time, even if the work is already done, so it never flickers. If the wait drags on, it escalates: first [a dim over content that is only stale](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-accessibility--docs#inert-beats-disabled-for-content-that-is-only-stale), then an overlay, then [a retry, offered only when it can help](?path=/docs/design-system-patterns--docs#trying-again-is-offered-only-when-it-can-help).

## Drawing what's coming

When the shape of what's coming is known before it arrives, the most honest indicator is that shape, drawn empty: rows for a table, cards for a list, bubbles for a conversation. It says what's coming and roughly how much, it holds the space so nothing jumps when the content lands, and it [draws the wait in the region that waits](?path=/docs/design-system-patterns--docs#a-wait-is-drawn-in-the-region-that-waits) rather than in a corner. A spinner floating over the page says only that something is happening somewhere.

<Illustration caption="An article before it arrives: the image, the heading, the author and the first lines are laid out as stand-ins before a word of them has loaded.">
	<PlaceholderDrawing />
</Illustration>

Where the shape isn't known, like a page that might show anything or a form whose result appears elsewhere, a plain indicator is right, and a placeholder would only be a guess.

A placeholder here is built from the same parts as the real thing, so the two line up and the swap is quiet. <Pull>Vary the sizes the way real content varies; a stack of identical bars reads as a broken layout, not as a wait.</Pull> The shapes are [decoration, hidden from assistive technology](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-accessibility--docs#decoration-is-aria-hidden), and the region that owns the wait announces it once.

## Technical rules

Every rule on this page, one sentence each, for looking up rather than reading. Open one for the details and the code.

<DetailsList>

<Details title="A move takes the shortest duration that still shows it">

The quickest duration is for popups, tooltips, hover and press, the next for dialogs, drawers and check marks, and a longer one for large surfaces. Explained in [Time and pace](#time-and-pace).

</Details>

<Details title="Each easing has one kind of move">

Standard for most moves, emphasised for a larger one like a dialog opening, decelerate for something arriving, accelerate for something leaving, and overshoot only for a small mark popping in. Shown in [Durations and easings](#durations-and-easings).

</Details>

<Details title="A duration or an easing is never a literal value">

Every transition reads its timing from the motion scale, so it matches every other move and can be retuned in one place. Shown in [Durations and easings](#durations-and-easings).

<DoDont>
<Example kind="do" caption="The timing comes from the scale.">

```scss
.popup {
	transition: opacity var(--motion-short)
		var(--motion-standard);
}
```

</Example>
<Example kind="dont" caption="A literal timing is a move of its own that nothing else matches.">

```scss
.popup {
	transition: opacity 0.15s ease;
}
```

</Example>
</DoDont>

</Details>

<Details title="Every transition runs only for someone who hasn't asked for less motion">

Guard it with the `prefers-reduced-motion` media query. Explained in [Time and pace](#time-and-pace).

<DoDont>
<Example kind="do" caption="The transition runs only for a user who has not asked for less motion.">

```scss
.popup {
	@media not (prefers-reduced-motion) {
		transition: opacity var(--motion-short)

	}
}
```

</Example>
<Example kind="dont" caption="An unguarded transition ignores a user who has asked for less motion.">

```scss
.popup {
	transition: opacity 0.15s ease;
```

</Example>
</DoDont>

<Details title="Pages that sit beside each other travel along that axis">

The browser runs this, not an animation mounted in script: the routed region is named, a move between siblings tags itself with a direction, and the stylesheet answers the tag. The direction belongs to the move, so it is read from the tab strip, which knows the order and where the user is. A destination outside the strip sends no direction and doesn't animate. Explained in [Moving through space](#moving-through-space).

</Example>

</Example>
</DoDont>

<Details title="A wait announces itself late, and then stays a moment">

A busy state waits out a delay before it appears, and once it has appeared it holds for a minimum duration. If the wait goes on, it escalates: a dim, then an overlay, then a retry. Feed the fetch state to the component that owns that bargain, and let it decide what the user sees. Explained in [The art of waiting](#the-art-of-waiting).

<DoDont>
<Example kind="do" caption="A refresh that resolves quickly shows nothing at all.">

```tsx

	<Stale.Content>{table}</Stale.Content>
</Stale>
```

</Example>
<Example kind="dont" caption="Every paging click flashes a spinner.">

```tsx
{
	query.isFetching ? <Spinner /> : table;
}
```

</Example>
</DoDont>

</Details>

<Details title="Every wait has an indicator, and that indicator keeps changing">

Every request, mutation, awaited handler and suspended boundary shows one, late but never not at all. The change runs on a loop for as long as the wait does: a `transition` plays once and stops, and so does `@keyframes` without `infinite`. Explained in [The art of waiting](#the-art-of-waiting).

<DoDont>
<Example kind="do" caption="The submit shows a spinner for as long as it is pending.">

```tsx
<Button
	type="submit"
	aria-busy={mutation.isPending || undefined}>
	{mutation.isPending ? <Spinner size={1} /> : null}
	{m.login_submit()}
</Button>
```

</Example>
<Example kind="dont" caption="The wait is invisible, so a slow network reads as a dead button.">

```tsx
<Form disabled={mutation.isPending}>
	<Button type="submit">{m.login_submit()}</Button>
</Form>
```

</Example>
</DoDont>

</Details>

<Details title="An indicator's size follows what is waiting, and it comes from the shared indicators">

An inline indicator, sized in `em`, for a control or a widget, so it sits on the line it interrupts. A prominent one for a whole view or a consequential load. Shown in [The art of waiting](#the-art-of-waiting).

<Details title="Under reduced motion, an indicator pulses instead of travelling">

Rotation and translation give way to a slow opacity pulse, which carries no vestibular cost. The same pulse is the only motion a skeleton needs. Explained in [The art of waiting](#the-art-of-waiting).
