# Iconography

An icon is a word's shorthand. A good one gets a familiar thing found faster: a magnifier where you search, a cog where the settings live. A poor one is a riddle. Icons help most when they stand beside the word they mean, and they are never the only way to learn what something does.

<Illustration caption="One outlined set, drawn on one grid with one line weight: enlarged, a document and a checklist still belong with the small glyphs around them.">
	<IconographyDrawing />
</Illustration>

## A picture beside a word

People recognise faster than they read. Once someone knows the cog means settings, their eye finds it before any label, and a row of icons turns into a row of landmarks. But only a handful of icons mean the same thing to everyone: search, close, add, settings. Past those, an icon is a guess, and two people guess differently.

So an icon keeps its word with it. The word says what the control does, and the icon makes it quicker to find the second time. Beside a word, the icon is decoration, and [decoration is hidden from screen readers](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-accessibility--docs#decoration-is-aria-hidden), because the word already says it. Where there is only room for the icon, the word moves somewhere else rather than disappearing: [an icon-only control always has a tooltip and an accessible name](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-interaction--docs#an-icon-only-control-always-has-a-tooltip-and-an-accessible-name).

## One family

Icons read as a set when they share everything but their subject: one grid, one line weight, one way of rounding a corner and drawing a curve. Mix two sets and the screen fills with small disagreements, a stroke a little heavier here and a corner a little rounder there, that nobody can name but everybody feels.

An icon's weight belongs to its surroundings too. Thin outlined glyphs sit easily among hairline borders, while solid ones look a shade heavier than everything around them. That difference is useful in its own right: an outlined icon that fills in when its item is chosen says "you are here" without a second colour or a change of words.

## Sized like a letter

An icon mostly sits in a line of text, so it is sized like a letter, as tall as the words beside it. A larger label brings a larger icon, and the pair stays balanced at every size. Where an icon stands on its own above a short label, it can afford to be a little larger, because it leads.

The glyph's size is about reading it. The size of what you tap is a separate question: [a touch target is larger than its glyph](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-responsiveness--docs#a-touch-target-is-larger-than-its-glyph), so a small icon never makes a small target.

## The set

This product draws its icons from Google's [Material Icons, in the outlined style](https://fonts.google.com/icons?icon.set=Material+Icons&icon.style=Outlined). Browse it there for a glyph and use the name it gives, because every glyph on that page is already available. The product draws only three glyphs of its own: the mark, the asterisk on a required field and the spinner.

<Specimen caption="A sample of the outlined set, at the size the bottom bar draws it, and the three glyphs the product draws itself: the mark, the required-field asterisk and the spinner.">
	<IconGallery />
</Specimen>

Outlined is the resting state everywhere, because its weight matches the hairline borders and one-pixel seams the rest of the system is built from. The filled style is kept for one job: the item you are on. A navigation entry fills its glyph while it is the current page, and goes back to outlined when it isn't. In the bottom bar, where each icon stands above a short label, the icons are half as large again as the text. And a button that carries a word [carries an icon as well](?path=/docs/components-button--docs#a-labelled-button-carries-an-icon-as-well).
