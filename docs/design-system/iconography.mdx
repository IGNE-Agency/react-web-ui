# Iconography

An icon is a word's shorthand. A good one gets a familiar thing found faster: a magnifier where you search, a cog where the settings live. A poor one is a riddle. Icons help most when they stand beside the word they mean, and they are never the only way to learn what something does.

The kit draws none of them. It ships no icon set; every place a component shows an icon is a slot the app fills from its own. What the kit keeps is everything around the glyph: its size, its colour, its label, and when it needs one.

## A picture beside a word

People recognise faster than they read. Once someone knows the cog means settings, their eye finds it before any label, and a row of icons turns into a row of landmarks. But only a handful of icons mean the same thing to everyone: search, close, add, settings. Past those, an icon is a guess, and two people guess differently.

So an icon keeps its word with it. The word says what the control does, and the icon makes it quicker to find the second time. Beside a word, the icon is decoration, and [decoration is hidden from screen readers](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-accessibility--docs#decoration-is-aria-hidden), because the word already says it. Where there is only room for the icon, the word moves somewhere else rather than disappearing: [an icon-only control always has a tooltip and an accessible name](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-interaction--docs#an-icon-only-control-always-has-a-tooltip-and-an-accessible-name).

## The app brings the icons

A component never decides which glyph an app uses, so it leaves a slot. Most slots are a part's children: the app puts its glyph inside the part, and a part given none renders nothing at all. A ready-made component that bundles several parts takes the glyph as a prop named for its job, such as `icon`, `closeIcon` or `expandIcon`.

```tsx
<Select.Icon>
	<ExpandMoreIcon />
</Select.Icon>

<Breadcrumbs.Separator>
	<ChevronRightIcon />
</Breadcrumbs.Separator>
```

A slot is optional when the control still works without the glyph: a chevron beside a label, a sort indicator, a magnifier in a search field. Left out, nothing is drawn. A slot is required when the glyph is all the control shows: the cross on a close button, the arrows of a stepper, the tick in a checkbox, the mark beside the chosen option. Without it the control would render blank, so the type check asks for it, next to the label that names the control.

## One family

Icons read as a set when they share everything but their subject: one grid, one line weight, one way of rounding a corner and drawing a curve. Mix two sets and the screen fills with small disagreements, a stroke a little heavier here and a corner a little rounder there, that nobody can name but everybody feels.

An icon's weight belongs to its surroundings too. Thin outlined glyphs sit easily among hairline borders, while solid ones look a shade heavier than everything around them. That difference is useful in its own right: an outlined icon that fills in when its item is chosen says "you are here" without a second colour or a change of words.

## Sized like a letter

An icon mostly sits in a line of text, so it is sized like a letter, as tall as the words beside it. A larger label brings a larger icon, and the pair stays balanced at every size. Where an icon stands on its own above a short label, it can afford to be a little larger, because it leads.

The kit sizes the slot, not the glyph. The part that holds an icon sets its size in `em` and its colour, and the glyph inherits both: an SVG drawn at `1em` in `currentColor` fits without a class of its own, and follows the control into its hover, disabled and invalid states, and into the dark theme.

The glyph's size is about reading it. The size of what you tap is a separate question: [a touch target is larger than its glyph](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-responsiveness--docs#a-touch-target-is-larger-than-its-glyph), so a small icon never makes a small target.

## The example set

These docs and the stories fill every slot from Google's [Material Icons, in the outlined style](https://fonts.google.com/icons?icon.set=Material+Icons&icon.style=Outlined), imported through SVGR from `@material-design-icons/svg`. It is an example, not a dependency: the package installs no icon set, and an app is free to pick any set that keeps to one family.

<Specimen caption="A sample of the outlined set, at the size the bottom bar draws it: glyphs that stand beside a word, and glyphs that fill a kit slot.">
	<IconGallery />
</Specimen>

A spinner, a radio's dot, a switch's thumb and the asterisk on a required field are shapes, not icons. The kit draws those itself, because they are part of how the control works rather than a picture of a word.

Whichever set an app picks, outlined is the resting state everywhere, because its weight matches the hairline borders and one-pixel seams the rest of the system is built from. The filled style is kept for one job: the item you are on. A navigation entry fills its glyph while it is the current page, and goes back to outlined when it isn't. In the bottom bar, where each icon stands above a short label, the icons are half as large again as the text. And a button that carries a word [carries an icon as well](?path=/docs/components-button--docs#a-labelled-button-carries-an-icon-as-well).

No kit module imports a glyph. An icon goes in a part's children, or in a ready-made component's `icon` or `<job>Icon` prop, and a slot left empty renders nothing. Lint rejects an icon-set or `?react` SVG import outside stories and docs. Explained in [The app brings the icons](#the-app-brings-the-icons).
