# Language

Words are the one part of an interface that everybody reads. A colour or a corner works on people without their noticing; a label, an error or an empty page is read word for word, by someone in the middle of a task who wants to get on with it. The best interface copy is plain, short and in the reader's own words, and it is the same every time it appears. A product that speaks more than one language writes every one of those words again, for readers with other words and other habits.

## The quiet voice

Nobody reads an interface the way they read a letter. They glance at a button, skim a heading, catch the first words of a message and move on. Text written for that kind of reading keeps its voice down: no capitals for emphasis, no exclamation marks, nothing clever. It sounds like a colleague who knows the work, not like an advertisement.

Capitals are the first thing to go. In sentence case only the first word and names take a capital, so a heading reads as calmly as a sentence and the eye goes straight to the words that matter. Title case gives every word a capital and makes them all compete, and many languages don't use it at all. Acronyms keep their capitals: BTW, IBAN, PDF.

Punctuation tells the reader what kind of text they are looking at. A sentence ends with a full stop, so an error, an empty state or a description reads as something being said. A label names a thing and stops: buttons, tabs, column headers and titles carry no full stop. Something that is still happening trails off in three dots, the way "Loading comments..." does.

Text that runs out of room is [folded onto a second line, never cut off](?path=/docs/design-system-foundation-type--docs#text-that-has-to-be-read-whole-is-folded-or-given-room-never-elided), because half a word is no word at all.

## Words that do things

A button is a promise. Its label tells the reader what will happen when they press it, so it is a verb, with the thing it acts on where that helps: "Download selection", "Add attachment". English puts the verb first; Dutch puts it last, "Selectie downloaden", and both are right. A control that means the same thing everywhere keeps one short word: Apply, Cancel, Remove.

"OK" and "Yes" make no promise at all. They confirm something without saying what, so the reader has to go back and read the question to find out what they agreed to. A dialog that asks whether to delete a comment answers with "Delete", not "OK".

A control's words reach people in more ways than one. The same name is read on screen and spoken by a screen reader, so [a control takes its name from exactly one place](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-accessibility--docs#every-control-has-a-name-from-exactly-one-mechanism), and [a tooltip never comes from the title attribute](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-interaction--docs#never-use-the-title-attribute).

## When something goes wrong

Someone reading an error is already stuck, and often a little worried. A good error answers the three questions they have, in order: what happened, whether it was something they did, and what they can do now. Said plainly, those three take the alarm out of almost any failure. A failure they can't fix themselves [says who can](?path=/docs/design-system-patterns--docs#trying-again-is-offered-only-when-it-can-help).

The server has its own message for every failure, and it is never shown. It is written for engineers, in one language, and it can change without warning. The product reads which failure it was and says it in its own words, in the reader's language.

## Said twice

A translation is a rewrite, not a word swap. Words carry more than one meaning, and the right one depends on where they stand: a single Finnish phrase can mean "six pieces", "six returns" or "the spruce is on fire". A translator handed a list of loose strings has to guess, so the people who translate need what the writer had: the screen, the situation, and the words the readers use in their own work. Where a field has settled on a word, that is the word, even when a dictionary offers a better one.

Each language also keeps its own manners. Some address the reader formally and some don't, and a few have several degrees in between. Capitals follow different rules: German capitalises every noun, and what looks like a mistake in one language is correct in the next. A product's voice is decided again for each language it speaks, and then kept, everywhere.

## Room to grow

The same sentence takes a different amount of room in every language. English is short. German and Dutch build long compound words, and the same label can come out half as long again, with no point at which it can be broken. A layout drawn around the shortest language breaks the moment the longest one arrives.

So a screen is designed for its longest language, with room for words to wrap onto a second line. Shrinking the type to squeeze a translation in is never the fix: the words get harder to read in exactly the language that already had the most of them. When a row of buttons won't fit, the answer is usually hierarchy, one clear action and quieter ones beside it, rather than smaller letters. The fitting itself is the type's job: [text that runs out of room folds rather than being cut off](?path=/docs/design-system-foundation-type--docs#text-that-has-to-be-read-whole-is-folded-or-given-room-never-elided), and [a box that must hold any word is as wide as the longest one](?path=/docs/design-system-foundation-type--docs#a-box-that-must-hold-any-text-is-sized-to-fit-its-longest-word).

</Illustration>

Word order moves too. English puts the verb at the start of a label and Dutch at the end; other languages put the name, the number or the date somewhere else again. A sentence built by gluing pieces together, "You have" plus a number plus "invoices", only works in the language it was glued in. A translator needs the whole sentence, with a slot for the value, to put that value where their own language wants it. Plurals are the same: English has two forms, some languages have one, and others have four or more, so the choice between them belongs to the translation, not to the code.

## Other places, other habits

Every region has its own way of writing the things that matter most. 24 July is 24-07 in one country and 7/24 in another; a thousand euro and twelve cents is 1.000,12 in one and 1,000.12 in the next; a distance is in kilometres or miles. Readers read their own conventions without thinking and misread anybody else's, so dates, numbers, money and units are written in the reader's own format. Names travel badly too: in some cultures the family name comes first, and a Dutch surname like "de Vries" is filed under its V.

Some languages are written in another direction altogether. Arabic and Hebrew run from right to left, and an interface in them is mirrored as a whole: navigation, alignment and the arrows that point forward and back. Other scripts need more height than Latin letters. Thai, Hindi and Arabic have tall letters, Chinese, Japanese and Korean are dense, and even Latin accents like the ones in Vietnamese stack above a capital. A product that may one day speak those languages writes its layout in terms of start and end rather than left and right, and gives its lines room above and below.

## Source language first

An app is written in its source language and may speak others beside it, and every word it shows exists in each. The longest of them decides the room: "Factuuroverzicht" stands where English says "Invoices", so a screen is drawn to hold the Dutch. Where all of them are written from left to right in Latin letters, nothing is mirrored, but the type still leaves room for the accents in a word like "financiën".

Within each language, one thing has one name. Readers learn the product's words the way they learn its screens, and a second name for the same thing makes them wonder whether it is a second thing. A client is never also a customer, and an invoice is never also a bill. The words are the ones its readers use in their own work.

<Illustration caption="Of all the words that could name the party that pays, the product uses one, and uses it everywhere.">
	<NameDrawing />
</Illustration>

| English        | Dutch       |
| -------------- | ----------- |
| invoice        | factuur     |
| client         | klant       |
| consultant     | adviseur    |
| purchase order | inkooporder |
| comment        | opmerking   |
| VAT number     | BTW-nummer  |

Each language joins words by its own rules. Dutch joins words into compounds, and after an acronym it joins them with a hyphen: BTW-nummer, KvK-nummer, PDF-factuur. English keeps them apart: VAT number, PDF invoice.

## Counting, dating and money

Numbers are read more carefully than anything else on a screen, because they are what people act on. A count is written in digits, "8 digits", "2 weeks", so it can be taken in at a glance. Dates, times and amounts are written the way each language writes them, but always in the app's time zone: an invoice made late in the evening in Amsterdam is dated that day, wherever in the world it is read. In a table [the euro sign moves into the column header](?path=/docs/components-data-table--docs#the-unit-is-named-once-in-the-header), "Amount (€)", and the cells hold the bare amount.

| What | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date | 24-07-2026 | 7/24/26 |
| Date and time | 24-07-2026, 11:30 | 7/24/26, 11:30 AM |
| Time | 15:30 | 3:30 PM |
| A range of days | 6 jan – 2 feb | Jan 6 – Feb 2 |
| Amount | € 12,34 | €12.34 |
| Amount in a table cell | 12,34 | 12.34 |
| Duration | 1 uur 30 min | 1 hr 30 min |
| File size | 1,5 kB | 1.5 kB |
| A value that is missing | — | — |

A format hint, such as "8 digits", belongs in a field's description: [a placeholder is never a field's only name](?path=/docs/design-system-principles-accessibility--docs#an-accessible-name-is-never-only-a-placeholder).
