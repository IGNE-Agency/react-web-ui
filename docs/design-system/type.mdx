# Type

Most of what a product says, it says in words, and type decides how those words are read: what comes first, what can be skimmed, what is only there to be pressed. When type is right nobody notices it. The reader just reads, and finds the next thing where they expected it.

<Illustration caption="Two families on one baseline. The white band is the x-height, where most of the reading happens.">
	<TypeDrawing />
</Illustration>

## What type does

Before anyone reads a word, the shape of the text has already told them where to look. A larger, heavier line reads as the title, a short line beside a field reads as its label, and a calm block of smaller text reads as something to settle into. That order is the hierarchy, and it comes from a handful of contrasts: size, weight, family and the space around a line.

The fewer of those contrasts a product uses, and the more consistently it uses them, the faster a reader learns them. A screen where every title is set a little differently makes the reader work out the order again each time.

<Illustration caption="Same letter, same size: the one set heavier, in the other family, still leads.">
	<HierarchyDrawing />
</Illustration>

Reading text has a comfortable width, too. Lines much longer than about seventy characters make the eye lose its place on the way back, and much shorter ones chop a sentence into pieces. Type and layout share that decision.

## A ladder of sizes, a set of jobs

Sizes come from a scale: a short ladder where each step is the one below it times a fixed ratio. A fixed ratio keeps the steps far enough apart that the difference between two of them is a decision anyone can see, and it lets the whole ladder be retuned at once.

The scale is raw material, though. What a screen asks for is a role, named after the job the text does: a heading, a label, body copy. A role settles the family, the weight, the size and the line height in one go, so they can't drift apart, and it keeps working when the ladder under it is retuned. It's the same idea as [a component reading a role rather than a step](?path=/docs/design-system-foundation-token-architecture--docs#a-component-reads-a-role-never-a-step), applied to type.

<Illustration caption="One letter at every step of a scale that grows by one ratio. The three in white are the steps the roles use, and the largest of them, a heading, is set in the heading family.">
	<LadderDrawing />
</Illustration>

## Two families

Type is set in two families. The heading family gives titles their character; the body family carries everything that is read or acted on.

The kit names no typeface and loads no font. Each family is a custom property, `--sys-font-family-heading` and `--sys-font-family-body`, and every role's `font` reads one of them. Both default to the system's own interface font, so a screen looks native until the app chooses otherwise, and the heading family follows the body family until it is given a face of its own.

<Specimen caption="Each family in the typeface its custom property names: the system's interface font, until an app sets one.">
	<TypefaceSpecimen />
</Specimen>

An app that has a typeface loads it itself, through `@font-face` or its font service, and names it on `:root`. One property swaps every role in that family.

```css
:root {
	--sys-font-family-body: "Inter", system-ui, sans-serif;
	--sys-font-family-heading:
		"Satoshi", system-ui, sans-serif;
}
```

## Roles

There are three groups of roles. Headings title dialogs and pages. Labels name the things you act on: buttons, tabs, nav items, field labels and chips. Body roles carry copy. A few of them are set in themselves below, with the family, weight, size and line height each resolves to.

## Holding still

Selecting, activating or hovering an item may change its weight and its colour. It never changes its size, family or line height: the item would grow, and every neighbour in the row or rail would shift to make room.

That's why each label role has a strong twin at exactly the same size, and a selected item takes that twin. Reaching for the next step up the ladder is the mistake the pairing exists to prevent.

## When words don't fit

<Beside>

There are three ways to force text into a box that's too small, and none of them is allowed. Clipping it with an ellipsis hides the part that tells an item apart from its neighbours. Shrinking it breaks the scale and gives the longest string the smallest type. Breaking it mid-word is the worst of the three, because the reader can't tell a fold from a spelling mistake.

What's left is folding where the language allows it: between words, and at a hyphenation point when a single word is longer than the box. The browser can only hyphenate when it knows which language it's reading, so the page's language follows the one the reader chose. <Pull>A word can't be relied on to have a hyphenation point, so a box that must hold any text is sized to fit its longest word.</Pull>

Copy that only fits at one length is a constraint on the copy, and the thing to change is usually how many items share the space, not how each one is set.

Everything that is read or acted on, labels and body text alike, is set in the body family. Shown in [Two families](#two-families).

<Details title="An app sets its typeface through the family properties on the root, and loads the font itself">

The token files name no family, and the kit loads no font. `--sys-font-family-body` defaults to `system-ui, sans-serif`, and `--sys-font-family-heading` follows it. Set either on `:root`, outside the kit's layers, where the roles resolve. Explained in [Two families](#two-families).

Labels cover buttons, tabs, nav items, field labels and chips. Shown in [Roles](#roles).

<Details title="A state never changes the type size">

A selected, active or hovered item may change its weight and its colour, never its size, family or line height. It takes its label role's strong twin, which has the same size. Explained in [Holding still](#holding-still).
